    Things to try

    1. Send coins from one node to another and follow the blue dot as the transaction spreads.
    2. Let two nodes mine at the same time and watch who wins the block.
    3. Take a node offline, let it mine alone, then bring it back. Its short fork gets replaced by the longer chain.
    4. Cut two connections to split the network in half. Both halves keep going until you reconnect them.
    5. Press “Try a double spend” and see which of the two payments ends up in a block.
    6. Open a block and change its nonce. The hash changes and the proof of work breaks.

    How it works

    Keys and addresses

    Every wallet holds real ECDSA key pairs (P-256). An address is the SHA-256 hash of a public key, shortened. Wallets use a new address for every coin they receive, so one person usually owns several addresses.

    Coins are UTXOs

    The chain stores no account balances. It only stores unspent outputs (UTXOs) of earlier transactions. Your balance is the sum of all UTXOs your keys can unlock.

    Transactions

    A payment spends whole coins as inputs and creates new outputs: one for the receiver and one for the change. Each input is signed with the key that owns it. Inputs minus outputs is the fee.

    Checking

    Every node checks every transaction on its own: is the signature valid, does the signer own the coin, is the coin still unspent, do the inputs cover the outputs? Only then does it keep it in its mempool and pass it on.

    Mining

    A miner packs transactions from its mempool into a block and tries nonce after nonce until the block's SHA-256 hash starts with enough zeros. The first transaction pays the miner 100 SIM plus all fees.

    Consensus

    Each block contains the hash of the previous block, so blocks form a chain. Nodes follow the chain with the most work. If two blocks compete, the next block decides, and the losing block's transactions go back to the mempool.

    How Chain-Check works

    1 · Attribution

    Analytics firms collect addresses that belong to known services: from seizures, court files, sanctions lists, test payments and their own investigations. Chain-Check has such a database for this chain.

    2 · Clustering

    When several addresses are spent together in one transaction, one person holds all their keys. This common-input heuristic groups thousands of addresses into wallets.

    3 · Tracing

    Chain-Check walks backwards from the deposit. At every transaction it splits the value over the inputs in proportion to their size (the haircut method) and stops at the first known service.

    4 · Exposure

    Direct exposure means the coins came straight from a risky service. Indirect exposure means there are hops in between. The closer and the larger the share, the higher the risk.

    5 · Behaviour

    Some patterns are suspicious by themselves: peel chains, money racing through many wallets, coins that wake up after years, and deposits split to stay under a limit (structuring).

    6 · Decision

    Low risk is accepted. Medium triggers questions about the source of funds. High means holding the deposit for due diligence. Severe means freezing it and reporting to the financial intelligence unit, without warning the customer.

    Limits

    Mixers, privacy coins and swaps to other chains break the trail. A trace is evidence, not proof: almost every old coin has touched something bad, which is why share and distance matter.

    In real life

    Tools such as Chainalysis, Elliptic or TRM Labs work this way at scale. In the EU, crypto service providers must also collect sender and receiver data for every transfer (Travel Rule) and report suspicious activity.

    Chain-Check is a teaching model. Scores, rules and thresholds are simplified and illustrative.
